The last lighthouse keeper of the northern isles

By Ana Ruiz · 12 min read

For thirty-one years, Magnus Halvorsen climbed the same hundred and twelve steps twice a day. He polished brass that nobody else would ever see, wound clockwork that had long since been replaced by electric motors, and wrote the weather in a ledger that the coastguard stopped collecting in 1998.

“Somebody has to know what the sea is doing,” he says, as if that settles it. On the morning I visit, the wind is gusting to fifty knots and the windows hum like a struck glass.

The machines tell you what happened. I can tell you what is about to happen.

A job that no longer exists

The automation of the lighthouses happened slowly and then all at once. By the late nineties, the keepers were gone from every station but this one, which survived on a technicality in a lease signed in 1911.

When the lease runs out next spring, Halvorsen will move to the mainland. He has not decided what to do with the ledgers. “Maybe a museum,” he says. “Maybe the stove.”
